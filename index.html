About the Case Study

Context, problem definition, analytical scope, and technical infrastructure.

1. Problem Statement & Motivation

The university years represent a critical developmental crossroad. As emerging adults leave parental supervision, they gain independent autonomy over food purchasing, preparation, and eating schedules. However, college students frequently operate under severe environmental friction: limited kitchen infrastructure in dormitories, demanding lecture and study timetables, financial constraints, and easy proximity to hyper-palatable quick-service fast food establishments.

Poor dietary choices during this window are correlated with cognitive fatigue, sleep disruption, elevated stress, and suboptimal academic outcomes. This project conducts a comprehensive, rigorous case study analyzing students' dietary behaviors, lifestyle patterns, and academic metrics, providing university stakeholders with actionable, data-informed intervention strategies.

Core Objectives

  • Conduct end-to-end data cleaning, anomaly detection, and principled imputation.
  • Formulate an empirical, transparent 0–100 Dietary Health Score.
  • Perform bivariate and multivariate statistical EDA answering 15 analytical questions.
  • Engineer 16 production Tableau calculated fields and 15 analytical worksheets.
  • Build 4 interactive Tableau dashboards addressing executive, behavioral, and academic questions.
  • Formulate 6 practical, institutional dietary strategies for campus health leaders.

Actionable Dietary Strategies

Evidence-based institutional policies and student behavioral nudges to cultivate healthy campus food environments.

📢 Institutional Notice: These dietary strategies are data-informed behavioral and organizational recommendations designed for university administrators, student affairs, and dining operators. They do not constitute personalized medical diagnosis or individual clinical nutrition prescriptions.
Strategy 1 • Morning Fuel

Grab-and-Go Morning Cafeteria Kiosks

Context: Only 37.9% of students consume breakfast regularly ($\ge 5$ days/week), while breakfast skippers display lower average GPAs (3.28 vs 3.52).

Implementation: Deploy mobile express breakfast kiosks across high-traffic academic corridors and lecture hall entrances between 7:30 AM and 10:00 AM. Provide whole fruit, Greek yogurt cups, oatmeal, and hard-boiled eggs covered under standard meal plan swipes.

Projected Target: Increase campus breakfast regularity rate from 37.9% to 65.0% over 2 academic semesters.
Strategy 2 • Fresh Produce Access

Subsidized Fresh Produce & "Farm-to-Quad" Popups

Context: Freshmen and on-campus dorm residents consume only 1.3 servings of fruit and 1.4 servings of vegetables daily, severely below USDA recommendations.

Implementation: Partner with regional agricultural cooperatives to host bi-weekly subsidized campus farmers markets accepting campus dining dollars. Introduce pre-portioned $5 fresh produce bundles in dorm convenience marts.

Projected Target: Elevate average daily student produce consumption from 3.48 to $\ge 5.0$ combined servings.
Strategy 3 • Hydration Infrastructure

Campus-Wide Smart Hydration Refill Stations

Context: 30.3% of students drink under 2.0L of water daily, with 6.4% in severe daily deficit (< 1.0L).

Implementation: Install touchless filtered water bottle refill stations in every residence hall floor and lecture facility. Distribute durable stainless-steel water bottles during freshman orientation featuring QR codes linking to campus hydration maps.

Projected Target: Achieve $\ge 88.0\%$ student population compliance with the 2.0L daily hydration guideline.
Strategy 4 • Culinary Empowerment

Residence Hall Kitchenettes & "Dorm Chef" Workshops

Context: Dorm students cook only 3.1 meals/week and consume fast food 3.4 days/week due to limited kitchen access and cooking inexperience.

Implementation: Retrofit residence common lounges with induction cooktops, microwave steam cookers, and communal refrigerators. Host student-led 20-minute cooking demos teaching 5-ingredient balanced meals on a budget.

Projected Target: Double dorm cooking frequency from 3.1 to 6.2 meals/week; reduce fast food frequency by 35%.
Strategy 5 • Late-Night Nourishment

Nutrient-Dense Late-Night Dining Hall Counterparts

Context: High-stress students consume 42% more soft drinks and fast food, especially during midterms and finals when commercial fast food delivery is the sole option.

Implementation: Extend campus library cafe hours until 1:00 AM offering warm grain bowls, protein wraps, vegetable soups, and herbal teas as high-nutrition alternatives to deep-fried commercial deliveries.

Projected Target: Cut high-stress student fast-food reliance from 3.5 to $\le 2.0$ days/week.
Strategy 6 • Grocery Financial Literacy

"Budget Bites": Financial Literacy & Grocery Navigation

Context: Students spending under $250/month exhibit a 2.4x produce deficit, misperceiving whole foods as universally unaffordable compared to processed items.

Implementation: Launch a digital campus app providing weekly grocery meal plans optimized under $40/week, unit price comparison tutorials, and batch cooking guides for frozen and canned produce.

Projected Target: Boost Dietary Health Scores among low-budget student cohorts by an average of +12.5 index points.

Methodological Framework

Rigorous documentation of data curation, cleaning audits, scoring models, and analytical guardrails.

Analytical Architecture & Pipeline

Data Collection (Raw N=1,255) ➔ Data Cleaning & Audit (Deduplication, Range Filtering, Cohort Median Imputation) ➔ Feature Engineering (Dietary Health Score 0–100) ➔ Exploratory Data Analysis (Pearson Correlations, Bivariate Testing) ➔ Tableau Engineering (16 Calculated Fields, 15 Worksheets) ➔ Interactive Dashboards (4 Modules) ➔ Institutional Strategies

1. Data Cleaning & Integrity Audits

The raw data generation incorporated deliberate real-world survey quality issues:

  • Deduplication: 5 identical student rows removed based on unique Student_ID.
  • Casing Harmonization: Rectified mixed capitalization across Gender and Residence_Type.
  • Domain Validation: Identified and rectified impossible ages (e.g. 145, -4), GPAs (>4.0, negative), and water volumes (>80L).
  • Principled Imputation: Cohort medians applied to missing budget, water intake, and breakfast responses to preserve true distribution variances.

2. Dietary Health Score Model

A multi-attribute scoring model (0–100 points) evaluating behavioral alignment with dietary guidelines:

  • Breakfast Regularity: Up to 15 points (0-1d: 3, 2-3d: 7, 4-5d: 11, 6-7d: 15).
  • Fruit Consumption: Up to 15 points (0: 2, 1: 6, 2: 11, $\ge 3$: 15).
  • Vegetable Consumption: Up to 15 points (0: 2, 1: 6, 2: 11, $\ge 3$: 15).
  • Hydration Volume: Up to 15 points (1.6–3.5L scored 12–15 pts).
  • Fast Food Moderation (Inverted): Up to 15 points ($\le 1$d: 15, $\ge 5$d: 1).
  • Soft Drink Moderation (Inverted): Up to 15 points ($\le 1$ can: 15, $\ge 7$ cans: 1).
  • Meal Regularity: Up to 10 points (Regular: 10, Somewhat: 6, Irregular: 2).

3. Scientific Guardrail: Correlation vs Causation

In observational survey analytics, establishing causation requires randomized controlled intervention trials (RCTs). A student with regular breakfast consumption may attain higher GPAs not solely due to morning glucose stabilization, but because regular breakfast co-occurs with earlier wake times, conscientious calendar management, consistent class attendance, and emotional stability. Throughout this project, all findings are framed strictly as empirical associations.

Comprehensive Project Report

Comprehensive Analysis and Dietary Strategies with Tableau: A College Food Choices Case Study

Institutional Data Analytics & Behavioral Strategy Research Report

Sample Size: N = 1,250 Validated University Students • Analytical Platform: Tableau 2023.2 & Python 3.6

1. Abstract

University students navigate an environment characterized by erratic academic schedules, social stress, financial limitations, and pervasive access to ultra-processed fast food. This case study analyzes a cross-sectional dataset of 1,250 validated student records to investigate dietary habits, lifestyle behaviors, and academic performance. Using exploratory data analysis, correlation testing, and Tableau dashboard suites, we engineered a transparent 0–100 Dietary Health Score. Our findings reveal that while 53.9% of students consume fast food $\ge 3$ days per week, only 37.9% consume breakfast regularly. Living arrangements represent the primary environmental determinant of dietary quality: dormitory residents cook an average of 3.1 meals weekly compared to 10.4 for students living with family. Breakfast regularity and sufficient nightly sleep (7.0–8.0 hours) exhibit positive associations with cumulative GPA ($r = +0.36$). Six institutional, data-informed intervention strategies are proposed to enhance student nutritional wellness without presenting clinical or diagnostic claims.

2. Introduction & Background

Transitioning to university life represents a critical window during which lifelong lifestyle habits are codified. As emerging adults assume full responsibility for grocery acquisition and culinary preparation, institutional meal environments and local food landscapes exert substantial influence. Emerging public health literature consistently highlights that college populations experience elevated rates of nutritional inadequacy, produce deficits, and energy drink reliance. Understanding these behavioral clusters is paramount for university administrations striving to bolster student retention, mental well-being, and academic success.

3. Problem Statement

While academic performance is conventionally analyzed through attendance and study hours, biological foundational pillars—specifically dietary quality, sleep hygiene, and physical activity—are frequently neglected in institutional analytics. University dining planners lack centralized, segment-specific insights regarding how dormitory housing, stress levels, and monthly food allowances restrict nutritious choices. This project solves that visibility gap through an integrated data analyst workflow and Tableau visualization platform.

4. Dataset & Data Cleaning Audit

The analysis investigated 1,250 validated records spanning 35 multidimensional attributes. Data cleaning incorporated systematic de-duplication (5 duplicate records purged), text case harmonization across categorical demographics, domain range boundary filtering (eliminating biologically impossible values such as negative ages or GPAs exceeding 4.0), and cohort median imputation for non-critical missing responses. Feature engineering derived the Dietary Health Score, Food Budget Utilization Rate, and discrete behavioral classification indicators.

5. Exploratory Data Analysis & Empirical Evidence

Statistical synthesis answers the core analytical questions:

  • Breakfast Habits: Only 37.9% of students eat breakfast regularly ($\ge 5$ days/week). Students in the "Excellent" GPA tier eat breakfast an average of 4.6 days/week, compared to 2.8 days for the "Below Average" tier.
  • Fast Food Reliance: 53.9% of the student cohort consumes fast food $\ge 3$ days weekly. Dormitory residents and Greek housing students exhibit the highest reliance (3.4 to 3.6 days/week).
  • Hydration Deficit: Average daily water intake is 2.40L, with 30.3% of the student body failing to attain the 2.0L minimum daily hydration benchmark.
  • Produce Consumption: Daily produce intake averages 1.63 fruit and 1.85 vegetable servings, falling short of national USDA guidelines.
  • Rest & Academics: Nightly sleep duration is positively correlated with cumulative GPA (Pearson $r = +0.36$). Students averaging 7.0–8.0 hours of sleep maintain a mean GPA of 3.52, whereas sleep-deprived peers (< 6.0 hours) average 3.21.
  • Stress Dynamics: High stress cohorts consume 42% more soft drinks and 35% more fast food meals than low stress cohorts, reflecting stress-induced coping behaviors.

6. Tableau Visual Analytics Architecture

Four specialized dashboards were constructed in Tableau:

  1. Executive Overview: High-level visibility into student counts, average GPA (3.42), dietary health score (63.6), and produce consumption across academic years.
  2. Dietary Behavior Deep-Dive: Granular multi-axis views comparing breakfast frequency, water intake distributions, and fast-food frequency across housing arrangements.
  3. Lifestyle & Academic Correlates: Scatterplots and regression trendlines illuminating sleep duration, exercise hours, and attendance against GPA.
  4. Dietary Strategy Roadmap: Cohort risk segmentation mapping targeted operational interventions against baseline vs. projected compliance metrics.

7. Data-Informed Dietary Strategies

In response to the identified behavioral vulnerabilities, six institutional interventions were developed:

  • Strategy 1: Morning Fuel Kiosks — Deploying express breakfast stations at lecture hall entrances to counter the 62.1% non-regular breakfast rate.
  • Strategy 2: Subsidized Produce Markets — Bi-weekly subsidized campus farmers markets and $5 produce grab bags in dorm mini-marts.
  • Strategy 3: Smart Hydration Refills — Installing touchless water refill stations across every dormitory floor and orientation water bottle distribution.
  • Strategy 4: Residence Lounge Kitchenettes — Equipping dorm lounges with induction cooktops and peer-led 20-minute culinary workshops.
  • Strategy 5: Nutrient-Dense Late-Night Offerings — Extending healthy dining hall service until 1:00 AM during midterms and finals.
  • Strategy 6: Budget Bites Navigation — Financial grocery literacy app teaching balanced meal preparation under $40 weekly.

8. Limitations & Analytical Guardrails

Several methodological boundaries must be underscored:

  • Self-Reported Survey Bias: Dietary recall and physical activity responses are subject to social desirability bias and imperfect recall.
  • Correlation vs. Causation: Observational associations do not establish biological or cognitive causation. Confounding factors such as baseline socioeconomic status, executive function, and mental health influence both dietary quality and GPA.
  • Non-Clinical Scope: The Dietary Health Score is an exploratory population indexing heuristic; it is neither a validated medical diagnostic tool nor clinical assessment.

9. Conclusion

This case study demonstrates the power of rigorous data analytics and visual storytelling with Tableau in uncovering the environmental determinants of university student wellness. By addressing housing infrastructure deficits and morning scheduling hurdles with data-informed operational strategies, university leadership can cultivate an academic ecosystem that nurtures both student health and academic achievement.
